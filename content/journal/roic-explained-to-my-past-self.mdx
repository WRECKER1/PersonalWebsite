If I could send one idea back to the version of me who was about to start a company, it would be this: growth is only worth something if the capital behind it earns more than it costs.

That sounds obvious written down. It was not obvious while I was chasing revenue.

## What it measures

Return on invested capital asks a plain question. For every rupee tied up in the business, in inventory, equipment, and money owed by customers, how much operating profit comes back each year after tax?

A business earning 25% on its capital and one earning 8% can report the same profit. They are not the same business. The first can fund its own growth. The second has to keep asking someone for money.

## Why it matters more than growth

Growth multiplies whatever is already there. If returns are above the cost of capital, growth creates value. If they are below it, growth destroys value, and faster growth destroys it faster.

> A business that needs a lot of capital to grow and earns little on it is a treadmill, not a compounding machine.

This is why two companies growing at the same rate can deserve very different valuations. The market is not paying for growth. It is paying for growth multiplied by the spread between return and cost.

## Where it misleads

Three places I have learned to be careful:

- **Acquired goodwill.** A company that buys its growth carries the purchase price on its balance sheet. Excluding goodwill tells you about the underlying business. Including it tells you about management's skill as a buyer. Both matter.
- **Old assets.** Heavily depreciated plant makes returns look high. Ask what it would cost to replace.
- **The average versus the margin.** A high average return says little about what the next rupee invested will earn. The incremental return is the one that drives future value.

---

None of this is original. But I notice that I only really understood it after I had personally run something where the capital was mine. Reading about a treadmill is different from being on one.
