For my first few attempts at research, I wrote in the order a report is read: thesis, business, financials, valuation, and finally a section called risks. By the time I reached that last section I had spent days building an argument, and the risks I listed were the ones that did not threaten it.

So I changed the order. Now the first page I write is the case against.

## Why order matters

Once a thesis is on paper, everything read afterwards gets sorted into evidence for it or noise. This is ordinary confirmation bias, and knowing its name does not prevent it. What helps is changing what gets anchored first.

If the bear case is written while I still know very little, it is written honestly. I have no position to defend. The questions it raises then shape what I look for in the filings.

## What a useful bear case looks like

It is not a list of things that could go wrong with any company. It is a specific argument made by an intelligent person who has looked at the same facts and reached the opposite conclusion.

A test I use: could I state the bear case well enough that someone who holds it would agree with my summary? If not, I do not understand it yet.

## The invalidation list

The companion habit is writing down, in advance, what would change my mind. Each [notebook entry](/ideas) now has a short list of observable conditions. If one of them occurs, the thesis is closed, however I feel about it at the time.

It is uncomfortable. That seems to be the point.
