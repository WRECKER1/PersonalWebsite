## Executive summary

Meridian Coatings is the fourth-largest decorative paints company in its market. It is neither the cheapest producer nor the best-known brand. What it has is a dense dealer network in smaller cities, built over two decades, and a tinting machine in almost every one of those shops.

The argument of this report is simple. In decorative paints, the dealer chooses what the painter recommends, and the painter chooses what the dealer stocks. A company that owns the shelf owns the sale. Meridian's network gives it more pricing power than its market share suggests, and that power is visible in how its gross margin behaved when raw-material costs rose in FY23.[^1]

<Callout title="The view in one paragraph">
A good business, not an obviously cheap one. The thesis depends on dealer economics staying attractive as larger, better-capitalised entrants push into the same towns. I would want to own it at a price that assumes competition intensifies, not one that assumes it fades.
</Callout>

## Investment thesis

Three ideas, in order of importance:

1. **Distribution is a fixed cost that competitors must also pay.** A new entrant needs to place a tinting machine, extend credit and guarantee next-day delivery to each dealer. That is expensive to replicate town by town.
2. **Mix is improving.** Premium emulsions and waterproofing carry higher margins than distemper and putty, and their share of revenue has risen each year.
3. **The balance sheet funds growth internally.** Capacity additions have been paid for from operating cash flow, with no equity dilution in the period studied.

## Industry overview

Decorative paints is a consumer business that behaves like a distribution business. Demand is tied to housing turnover and repainting cycles, and the product is bought infrequently by people who rely on a painter's advice.

Three features define the industry's economics. Raw materials, mostly crude-linked, make up more than half of the cost of goods. Dealers carry inventory on credit from the manufacturer. And tinting machines mean a dealer can offer thousands of shades from a handful of base paints, which makes shelf space both scarce and sticky.

## Business model analysis

Meridian sells through roughly 31,000 dealers, of which about 26,000 have a tinting machine installed. The company owns the machines and lends them to dealers, which ties the dealer to Meridian's bases.

| :-- | --: | --: | :-- |
| Premium emulsions | 38% | 46% | Growing fastest; brand-led |

| Waterproofing & construction chemicals | 14% | 42% | Newer; sold through the same dealers |
| Industrial coatings | 7% | 24% | Sub-scale; strategic value unclear |

The interesting line is waterproofing. It uses the existing network, needs little incremental capital, and is recommended by the same painters. If it continues to grow, it raises the return on the distribution asset without adding to it.

The numbers support the story, with one caveat on working capital.

Two observations. First, the FY23 margin compression was shallower than I would expect for a company of this size, and it reversed within a year. That is what pricing power looks like in the accounts. Second, receivable days have crept up from 41 to 49 over the period.[^2] Dealer credit is part of the moat, but it is also where a moat quietly gets more expensive to maintain.

## Valuation

I approached valuation from two directions and deliberately did not try to reconcile them to a single number.

**Earnings power.** On normalised margins of around 18% and maintenance capital spending of roughly ₹200 crore, the business generates free cash of about ₹650 crore a year before growth investment.

**Reverse DCF.** Rather than forecast, I asked what the current price assumes. At the sample price used in this report, the market is implying roughly 11% revenue growth for a decade with stable margins. That is close to what the company has delivered, which means the price already assumes the moat holds.

| Scenario | Revenue growth (10 yr) | Terminal margin | Value vs. current price |
| :-- | --: | --: | --: |
| Competition bites | 7% | 15.5% | −32% |
| Base | 10% | 18.0% | −4% |
| Mix improves | 12% | 19.5% | +27% |

The asymmetry is not compelling at this price. The quality is.

## Risks and catalysts

**Risks**

- Well-funded entrants offering dealers higher margins and free machines.
- A sustained rise in crude-linked input costs that cannot be passed through.
- Receivables continuing to lengthen as the company defends shelf space with credit.

**Catalysts**

- Waterproofing reaching scale and lifting blended margins.
- Evidence that new entrants are slowing dealer additions.
- A decision on the sub-scale industrial coatings segment.

## Conclusion

Meridian is a business I would like to own and do not yet want to buy. The moat is real and is visible in the numbers, but the price leaves little room for the scenario in which competition is worse than the last five years suggest. It goes on the watchlist with a clear trigger: dealer additions and receivable days, tracked each quarter.
