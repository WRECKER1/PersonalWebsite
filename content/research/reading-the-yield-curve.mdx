## Executive summary

The yield curve is often quoted and less often understood. This note sets out a framework for reading it: what each part of the curve reflects, why the slope carries information, and where equity investors tend to over-interpret it.

## Investment thesis

The curve is not a forecast. It is a price, set by people with different horizons and constraints. Its usefulness comes from decomposing that price into expected policy rates and a term premium, and from being honest about which of the two is moving.

## Industry overview

Short-dated yields are anchored by the central bank's policy rate. Long-dated yields reflect the expected path of that rate over time, plus compensation for bearing duration risk. The difference between them, the slope, therefore mixes a view on policy with a view on risk.

## Business model analysis

It helps to think about who is on each side. Banks borrow short and lend long, so a steep curve widens their margins. Insurers and pension funds need long-dated assets regardless of price. Foreign investors respond to rate differentials and currency expectations. Each group pushes on a different part of the curve.

## Financial analysis

| Curve shape | Usual interpretation | A sensible caution |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Steep | Growth and inflation expected to rise | May simply reflect heavy government borrowing |
| Flat | Policy tight relative to expectations | Can persist for a long time |
| Inverted | Markets expect rate cuts | Timing between inversion and slowdown varies widely |

## Valuation

For equities, the curve matters through the discount rate. Long-duration assets, companies whose cash flows sit far in the future, are most sensitive to the long end. A change in the term premium can move their value without any change in the business.

## Risks and catalysts

The main risk in using this framework is false precision. The decomposition into expectations and term premium is model-dependent, and different models disagree.

## Conclusion

Use the curve as a check on your own assumptions, not as a signal. If a valuation only works at a discount rate the bond market does not believe in, that is worth knowing.
