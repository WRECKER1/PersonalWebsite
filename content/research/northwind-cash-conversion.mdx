## Executive summary

Northwind Logistics reports fast-growing profit. Its cash flow statement tells a quieter story. This note reconciles the two and asks whether the gap is the temporary cost of growth or a sign that revenue is being recognised ahead of the cash that should follow it.

## Investment thesis

There is no thesis yet. This is a diagnostic exercise: before forming a view on value, establish whether reported earnings can be trusted as a measure of economic earnings.

## Industry overview

Third-party logistics providers manage warehousing and transport for clients on contract. Customers are large and pay slowly. Suppliers, mostly small fleet owners, need to be paid quickly. The business therefore funds its customers' working capital as a matter of course.

## Business model analysis

Northwind has grown by winning large enterprise contracts. Larger customers bring volume and longer payment terms. The question is how much of the growth has been bought with credit.

Receivable days have risen by more than a month while payable days have shortened. Two further items deserve attention: unbilled revenue has grown faster than billed revenue, and the provision for doubtful debts has not risen in proportion to receivables.[^1]

## Valuation

Valuing this business on a multiple of reported earnings would overstate what an owner actually receives. On a free cash flow basis, the yield at the sample price is under 2%.

## Risks and catalysts

- **Risk:** a large customer dispute forcing a write-down of old receivables.
- **Risk:** borrowing to fund working capital as growth continues.
- **Catalyst:** a visible improvement in collections over two or more quarters.

## Conclusion

The earnings are real in an accounting sense and not yet real in a cash sense. Until receivable days stabilise, this stays in the notebook under monitoring.

[^1]: Unbilled revenue is revenue recognised for work done but not yet invoiced. Persistent growth in this line is worth questioning directly with management.
